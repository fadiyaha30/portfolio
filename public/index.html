A little about me

An developer with curiosity
for what comes next.

I grew up watching Doraemon, and its futuristic gadgets sparked my curiosity about technology and Japan. That interest led me to study Electronics and Instrumentation at Universitas Gadjah Mada, where I learned to code and discovered how much I enjoyed building things with software. For my thesis, I combined YOLO car detection with an automatic barrier prototype, bringing software and hardware together.

Today, I’m living in Japan and working as a full-stack software developer, developing web applications across frontend and backend. Web development wasn’t a major focus of my degree, so much of my knowledge came from hands-on experience—researching, experimenting, and learning from the people around me. It feels special to see a childhood interest become part of my everyday life.

My work has taught me to think beyond whether the code works. I consider how people will use a feature, whether a button’s purpose is clear, and how a change might affect their experience. Working with a team has also helped me communicate my ideas, listen to different perspectives, and find solutions together.

I want to keep growing as a software developer, building on my web development experience while exploring app development and other areas of software. Using AI in my daily work has also made me curious about how it works and how I could apply it in what I build.

I’m excited to keep learning and exploring what I can build next!

Academic projects

ACADEMIC PROJECT · UNDERGRADUATE THESIS

Car Detection Simulation
Using the YOLO Algorithm

A prototype that connects computer vision with hardware: detecting toy cars and controlling an automatic barrier using an ESP32-CAM, Arduino, and a servo.

PythonYOLOv5Arduino C/C++ESP32-CAM
Explore the approach +

Compared YOLOv5s and YOLOv5l using a dataset of 60 toy cars. YOLOv5l achieved the higher reported accuracy (78.1% versus 76.5%) and was selected for the barrier prototype. Camera images were processed for detection, then signals were sent to the Arduino to open and close the barrier.

This was a controlled simulation using toy cars.
